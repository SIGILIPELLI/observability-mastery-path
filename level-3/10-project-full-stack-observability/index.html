    Project: Full-Stack Observability for a Real Request¶

    What was run¶

    The full real two-service OpenTelemetry system from this level — service_a_otel.py and service_b_otel.py, both emitting real metrics, real structured logs, and real propagated traces — sent 20 real requests, with Service B's genuine ~20% random failure rate producing real errors. One specific real failed request was then traced completely across all three real signals, across both real services, end to end.

    The real metric: aggregate signal¶

    curl -s localhost:9502/metrics | grep service_b_compute_total

    service_b_compute_total{result="error"} 4.0
service_b_compute_total{result="ok"} 16.0

    4 of 20 real requests failed — a real, aggregate 20% error rate.

    The real log: one specific instance, with its trace ID¶

    grep "compute_failed" b_final.log | head -1

    {"trace_id": "7ed9d1d616536edbcaa81afe5d02c846", "event": "compute_failed", "reason": "simulated transient fault"}

    The real, full cross-service trace for that exact request¶

    Service A's real span:
  name="handle"  trace_id=0x7ed9d1d6...  span_id=0x6ff387c0f0144f4f

Service B's real span:
  name="GET /compute"  trace_id=0x7ed9d1d6...  parent_id=0x6ff387c0f0144f4f  status_code="ERROR"

    Service B's top-level span's parent_id is exactly Service A's handle span's span_id — real, cross-process proof the two services' independently-created spans belong to the same real request, and Service B's span is correctly marked ERROR. Three signals, two separate real processes, one real request: a real 20%-of-20 aggregate count, a real log line naming this specific trace and its reason, and a real trace confirming exactly where in the real two-service call chain it failed.

    How It Actually Works¶

    This project is the direct combination of this level's real building blocks: the trace propagation mechanism from Lesson 2 (the real shared trace_id across process boundaries), the OTel-native metrics from Lesson 3, the structured logging and correlation pattern from Lesson 6, all running simultaneously across a real multi-service system rather than one single process. The real, complete chain demonstrated above — aggregate count down to one specific instance down to its exact structural location in a cross-service call — is the actual, practical payoff every earlier lesson in this level was building toward individually.

    It's also worth being honest about what this capstone does not cover, consistent with this level's stated scope limits: no real Collector batching and forwarding this data to a production backend (Lesson 4's real limitation), no real sampling applied to reduce volume at scale (Lesson 5's real tradeoff), and no cardinality discipline specifically enforced on these particular metrics' labels (Lesson 7's real warning) — all genuinely applicable to a production version of this same system, deliberately left out here to keep the core, real mechanism — one request, three correlated signals, two real services — as clear and directly inspectable as possible.

    Common mistakes¶

    • Building rich instrumentation across multiple services without a real, verified test that the correlation actually works end to end — this project's real cross-service parent_id match is exactly the kind of direct verification that confirms propagation isn't silently broken somewhere in the chain.
    • Treating "we have metrics, logs, and traces" as equivalent to "we have observability" — the real value, as this entire capstone demonstrates, comes specifically from being able to move between the three signals for one real event, not from each existing independently and never being cross-referenced.
    • Stopping instrumentation at the first service in a chain and assuming it "covers" the whole request — this project's real trace only tells the complete story because both services propagated and recorded it consistently; a gap at either end would have left an incomplete picture.

    Exercise¶

    Add a third real service to this chain, have it fail independently with its own real random error rate, and extend this project's real correlation exercise: find one real request that failed at the third service (not the second), and confirm you can trace it back through all three real services' spans, logs, and metrics, the same way this lesson did for a two-service failure — proving the pattern holds as the real chain gets longer, not just for the simplest two-hop case.
